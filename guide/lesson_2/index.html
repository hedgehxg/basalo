Lesson 2 — How to make a sentence

Basalo sentences are very free in the order that words go in. However, there are a few rules:

  1. The person doing an action, or the subject, has a before it,
  2. The person experiencing an action, or the object, has a preposition before it (usually e),
  3. And modifying words (adjectives, adverbs) will always come after what they modify.

If a or e is the first word of a sentence, it should be dropped!

Statements

With these rules, let's see a few example sentences:

Basalo English
me yo kan e fon la naya! I have a new phone!
yo dolna a le He is running.
wangu a salam yo ide. The man is going to the market.
ca la enyum yo esa a me. I'm drinking some hot tea.

You'll notice that the verbs in these sentences all have yo before them. This word describes an action that's unfinished or still happening, and always goes directly before the verb.

  • me yo sin e salam. — I see a person. / I'm seeing a person.
  • me a les yo ogop cu? — Can they help me?
  • yo esa a mes e kafe! — We're drinking some coffee!

You'll also see the word la in the phrases fon la naya and ca la enyum. This word goes right before any adjectives that modify a noun or noun phrase.

  • yay la imut - a good performance
  • pole la ik pasa - a small, peaceful field
  • gidas lua la aga - two big houses

Questions

Yes-no questions are made by putting the question particle cu at the end of a sentence; sort of like a spoken question mark.

  • le yo kan e kitap. — He has a book.
  • le yo kan e kitap cu? — Does he have a book (yes or no)?

Content questions (questions about "who", "what", "where", etc.) are made by putting the quetion particle ki at the end of a sentence.

  • yo ista a me e bananas! - I like bananas!
  • yo ista a te e kel ki? - What do you like? (You like what?)

Negatives

In general, you can make a sentence negative by putting ma right after the verb:

  • aman yo nalu. — The water is rippling.
  • aman yo nalu ma. — The water is not rippling.

You can negate a specific word in a sentence by putting ma after it.

  • inaki ma yo esa e owoce. — The dog isn't eating the fruit.
  • inaki yo esa e owoce ma. — The dog isn't eating the fruit.

Wait, how do you say "the" and "a"?

You don't! Or, more precisely, there's no dedicated word for either of these. If you want to refer to one thing, you could use un, meaning "one":

  • mama — the parent, a parent
  • mama un — one parent

And if you want to refer to some specific object, you could use lel, which means "this" or "that". We'll become more acquainted with words related to lel in Lesson 5, as it's one of the doskamolas:

  • kin — the plant, a plant
  • kin lel — that specific plant

Remember, don't get confused with the word la; it's just there to separate adjectives from nouns:

  • oto la naya — a new car, the new car
  • umal la idaman — a red apple, the red apple

Using lel versus le

Both lel and le are very versatile words which we'll see a lot in the coming lessons. In short, le means "he" or "she", while lel can mean "this" or "that".

  • le yo meti e kitap lel. — He's putting down that book.
  • yo sin a te e le cu? — Do you guys see her?

When we use lel after a noun earlier in a sentence, we can refer to that whole noun by just using lel, like so:

  • salam lel, lel e kitap meya yo kan cu? — That guy, does he have my book?
  • te e yoku lel la naya yo sin? lel yo e kul aga. — Are you seeing that new game? It's super cool.

Some words to start with

Now that we know how to put sentences together, here's a few words you can play around with:

  • me, te, le — I/me, you, he/she
  • mes, tes, les — we/us, you all, they
  • lel — this, that
  • salam — person, man, woman
  • mama — parent, mother, father
  • kitap — to read; book
  • sin — to see, look at
  • esa — to eat, to drink; food, beverage
  • yati — to speak, talk; speech
